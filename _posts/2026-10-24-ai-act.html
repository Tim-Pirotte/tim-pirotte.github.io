Recently, a Tech&Meet was held by the company Mr. Franklin to talk about the EU AI Act. What is a Tech&Meet you ask? It is an event hosted at Howest, the school I currently go to, where people come and talk about trending topics in the tech industry. This year those are AI and Cybersecurity.

Let's go over the basics.

Who is Who?

The act puts every company along the supply chain in a certain category. That category specifies the regulations and requirements the company has to follow.

Provider

Providers develop the AI-system. They have the most responsibilities when it comes to following regulations. Even open source projects have to follow some requirements.

Deployer

Deployers use or implement the system. They have to check if the provider is in compliance with the act, employees have enough AI literacy and inform end-users of the use of AI. When the model is classified as high-risk then they also need to install human oversight. Once a deployer does rebranding of a model, substantially changes it or uses it in a non-inteded way then it is considered a provider.

Importer

Importers bring a model of a non-EU provider on the EU market. They are the contact point for EU-authorities.

Distributers

Distributers make an AI-system available on the EU market but on a different part on the supply chain than importers. An example of this is an app store. They have similar responsibilities to deployers.

The Risk Piramid

All models are either classified in different risk categories or considered general purpose AI (GPAI). GPAI will be discussed later.

Unacceptable Risk

Applications falling under this category are illegal. There are a few exception though.

Here is a list of everything currently considered illegal:
  • Cognitive behavioral manipulation
  • Social scoring
  • Generating intimate images without consent
  • Biometric categorization of People
  • Real-time and remote biometric identification systems
  • Predicting the risk that someone might commit a crime only using profiling
  • Emotion detection at work or in educational institutions
  • Scraping facial recognition data
These are the exceptions:
  • Emotion detection for medical and security purposes
  • Post-remote biometric identification for law enforcement
  • Real-time biomentric identification for law enforcement under specific circumstances
High Risk

Systems falling under this category are legal but fall under strict requirements. This is because there expected to negatively affect safety or fundamental rights. All of the models fall under two categories. Ones that fall under EU product safety legislation such as planes, cars, toys and elevators and ones that are used in specific domains.

Those are the following domain:
  • Education
  • Employment
  • Law enforcement
  • Migration, asylum and border control
  • Critical infrastructure
  • Justice and democratic processes
The requirements for providers are the following:
  • Having a risk-management and system
  • Applying data governance
  • Providing technical documentation
  • Adding logging to the system
  • Creating the system in such a way that human oversight is possible
  • Ensuring that the system is accurate, robust and secure
  • Providing instructions to deployers
  • Having a quality-management and system
  • Registering the system in an EU-database
  • Receiving a CE marking
Limited Risk

These systems have transparency and copyright requirements.

System types:
  • Chatbots in contact with people
  • Content generation
  • Expression detection
  • Biometric categorisation
requirements:
  • Watermarking generated content
  • Putting in safeguards against illegal content generation
  • Publishing a summary of copyrighted data used during training
Minimal Risk

Systems not falling under one of the above categories are minimal risk. They don't have any requirements.

General Purpose AI

GPAI systems have significant generality and can perform a wide range of tasks. When the system passes a treshhold then it is also considered to have systemic risk which adds more requirements. Currently the treshhold is 1025 floating-point operations per second, which does make me wonder what happens if a model is trained with a different format for fractions such as posits.
