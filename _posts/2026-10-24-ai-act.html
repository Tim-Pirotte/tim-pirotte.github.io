Disclaimer: This is not legal advice. A high level overview of the EU AI Act will be provided but the laws are more nuanced and complicated than that so take everything with a grain of salt.

Recently, a Tech&Meet was hosted by the company Mr. Franklin, to talk about the EU AI Act. What is a Tech&Meet you ask? It is an event hosted at Howest, the school I currently go to, where people come and talk about trending topics in the tech industry. This year those are AI and Cybersecurity.

Let's go over the basics.

Who is Who?

The act puts organisations along the AI supply chain in certain categories. That category specifies the regulations and requirements the company has to follow.

Provider

Providers develop the AI-system. They have the most responsibilities when it comes to following regulations. Even open source projects have to follow some requirements.

Deployer

Deployers use or implement the system. They have to check if the provider is in compliance with the act, employees have enough AI literacy and inform end-users of the use of AI. When the model is classified as high-risk then they also need to install human oversight. Once a deployer rebrands a model, substantially changes it or uses it in an unintended way, then it is considered a provider.

Importer

Importers bring a model of a non-EU provider on the EU market. They are the contact point for EU-authorities.

Distributors

Distributors make an AI-system available on the EU market but on a different part on the supply chain than importers. An example of this is an app store. They have similar responsibilities to deployers.

The Risk Pyramid

All models are either classified in different risk categories or considered general purpose AI (GPAI). GPAI will be discussed later.

Unacceptable Risk

Applications falling under this category are illegal. There are a few exception, though.

Here is a list of everything currently considered illegal:
  • Cognitive behavioral manipulation
  • Social scoring
  • Generating intimate images without consent
  • Biometric categorization of people
  • Real-time and remote biometric identification systems
  • Predicting the risk that someone might commit a crime only using profiling
  • Emotion detection at work or in educational institutions
  • Scraping facial recognition data
These are the exceptions:
  • Emotion detection for medical and security purposes
  • Post-remote biometric identification for law enforcement
  • Real-time biometric identification for law enforcement under specific circumstances
High Risk

Systems falling under this category are legal but fall under strict requirements. This is because they are expected to negatively affect safety or fundamental rights. High-risk systems fall into two main categories. Ones that fall under EU product safety legislation such as planes, cars, toys and elevators and ones that are used in specific domains.

Those are the following domains:
  • Education
  • Employment
  • Law enforcement
  • Migration, asylum and border control
  • Critical infrastructure
  • Justice and democratic processes
The requirements for providers are the following:
  • Having a risk-management system
  • Applying data governance
  • Providing technical documentation
  • Adding logging to the system
  • Creating the system in such a way that human oversight is possible
  • Ensuring that the system is accurate, robust and secure
  • Providing instructions to deployers
  • Having a quality-management system
  • Registering the system in an EU-database
  • Receiving a CE marking
Limited Risk

These systems have transparency and copyright requirements.

System types:
  • Chatbots in contact with people
  • Content generation
  • Expression detection
  • Biometric categorisation
Requirements:
  • Watermarking generated content
  • Putting in safeguards against illegal content generation
  • Publishing a summary of copyrighted data used during training
Minimal Risk

Systems not falling under one of the above categories or GPAI are considered minimal risk. They don't have any requirements in this act.

General Purpose AI

GPAI systems have significant generality and can perform a wide range of tasks. When the system passes a treshold then it is also considered to have systemic risk which adds more requirements. Currently the treshhold is 1025 floating-point operations, which makes me wonder what happens if a model is trained with a different format for fractions such as posits.

These are the requirements for GPAI systems:
  • Create technical documentation
  • Provide documentation to downstream providers
  • Make a policy to comply with the EU Copyright Directive
  • Publish a summary of used training data

An open source model only has to comply with the last two requirements unless it has systemic risk.

These are the additional requirements for models with systemic risk:
  • Model evaluations
  • Adversarial testing
  • Assess and mitigate systemic risks
  • Report serious incidents
  • Install adequate cybersecurity

Timeline

Not all of these things were mandatory at once. They are doing a gradual rollout.

The timeline:
  1. 1 August 2024: The AI Act entered the law but without enforcement
  2. 2 February 2025: Ban on unacceptable risk systems and AI literacy obligations
  3. 2 August 2025: Rules for GPAI
  4. 2 August 2026: Enforcement of most rules and general transparency obligations
  5. 2 December 2026: Banning of deepfakes and intimate content
  6. 2 August 2027: Legacy GPAI systems must be fully compliant
  7. 2 December 2027: High-risk requirements for standalone systems
  8. 2 August 2028: High-risk requirements for regulated systems such as toys
  9. 2 August 2030: All legacy AI systems must be fully compliant

Fines

So what does it cost a company to not comply? Well that depends on the type of offence. There is a fixed amount or a percentage of yearly revenue per fine and the highest of those two has to be paid. These numberss are upper bounds.

These are the different fines:
  • Prohibited practices: 7% or 35 000 000
  • Other AI act infringements: 3% or € 15 000 000
  • Incorrect, incomplete or misleading information: 1% or € 7 500 000
